## What I Rejected

### Human value as an ability AI does not have yet

I rejected the idea that human value comes from abilities that AI has not mastered yet. 
At first, this seemed like a reasonable answer: humans could preserve their value by focusing on creativity, emotion, or judgment. However, I realized that any ability that can be clearly defined can potentially be analysed, systematised, and eventually learned by AI. Defining humanity through a temporary advantage only creates an endless race.

---

### Game feel as a permanent human advantage

I rejected the idea that game feel would remain a uniquely human domain. 
Although concepts such as "heaviness," "fluidity," and "danger" appear subjective, they can potentially be translated into parameters, timing, physics, animation, and interaction systems. Therefore, game feel should not be treated as the final boundary separating humans and AI.

---

### Programming as merely a production skill

I rejected the idea that programming is valuable only because it allows designers to produce things more efficiently. 
If programming is only a faster way of creating code, then AI will eventually surpass humans. Instead, I began to see programming as a way to understand systems, construct rules, and explore how interactive worlds are built.

---

### Human vs. AI as the main framework

I rejected the framework of constantly comparing human abilities with AI abilities. 
This question assumes that human value should be measured by the same standards as machine performance. Influenced by Heidegger's idea of Enframing, I realised that the problem may not be finding what humans can do better, but questioning why productivity should define human value in the first place.

---

### The claim that AI can never have emotion or consciousness

I rejected the absolute claim that AI will never experience emotion or consciousness. 
Although human experience is unique, it is difficult to prove what AI may or may not become in the future. Instead of arguing that AI can never experience, I refined my argument: simulation and lived experience are not necessarily the same thing.

---

### Artistic value as simply output quality

I rejected the idea that the value of art comes only from the quality of the final product. 
If artistic value is only based on technical perfection, AI-generated works may eventually surpass human creations. Through Benjamin's discussion of mechanical reproduction, I explored how artworks can carry meaning through their relationship with a particular creator and historical context.

---

### Creativity as the ability to produce something novel

I rejected the definition of creativity as simply generating something new. 
Since AI can already create novel combinations and possibilities, novelty alone cannot define human creativity. Instead, I began to focus on intention, context, and responsibility behind creation.

---

### Choice as merely selecting the objectively best option

I rejected the idea that human choice is simply choosing the optimal solution. 
If AI can evaluate possibilities more efficiently, then optimisation can also become automated. Human choice is meaningful because it involves commitment, personal values, and responsibility rather than only calculation.



## What I Kept

### Programming as a way of thinking

I kept programming because I realised that coding is not only a production method. 
Following Papert's idea of computational thinking, programming allows designers to understand systems, test ideas, and construct possible worlds. For a game designer, this understanding is essential.

---

### Games as systems of rules, interaction, and emergence

I kept the idea that games are not only collections of visual assets or narratives. 
Through Salen and Zimmerman's theory of game design, I recognised games as systems created through rules, interactions, and player experiences. Programming therefore becomes a way to understand and shape these systems.

---

### Art as connected to a particular human life

I kept the idea that artworks gain meaning through their connection to human experience. 
This does not mean AI-generated works have no value, but that human-created works can carry traces of a specific person's history, choices, and existence.

---

### Human finitude as an important context of creation

I kept human finitude as a foundation of artistic meaning. 
Humans have limited time, memories, and experiences. This limitation is not simply a weakness; it creates the unique context from which individual perspectives emerge.

---

### Choice as commitment rather than optimization

I kept the idea that artistic choice is not only about finding the best solution. 
A creator chooses something because it reflects their values, perspective, and attachment. Choice means taking responsibility for one possibility among many.

---

### Creation as a process of self-formation

I kept the idea that creation changes the creator themselves. 
The purpose of creating is not only producing an external object, but also understanding oneself through the process.

---

### The artist as someone who chooses and takes responsibility

I kept this new definition of the artist. 
In a future where production becomes increasingly automated, artists may become less like manufacturers and more like people who define direction, meaning, and responsibility.

---

### Learning as part of becoming oneself

I kept learning itself as valuable, even when AI can perform the same task. 
Learning programming, drawing, or design is not only about acquiring skills; it is also a process through which people develop their own ways of thinking and seeing the world.

---

### Authentic and valid literature retrieved by AI

I kept the use of AI as a research assistant rather than an authority. 
AI helped me discover related theories and references, but I did not accept its suggestions without verification. The final selection of literature was based on whether the sources were academically valid and whether they genuinely supported my argument.
