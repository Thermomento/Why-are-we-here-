# Why Are We Here?

As a designer, why do I need to learn programming?

---

## Before Answering, Who Am I?

During my undergraduate studies, I started using AI tools regularly. At that time, AI still felt limited. It could answer simple questions, generate basic images, or provide imperfect code, but there was still a clear boundary between what humans could do and what machines could not.

However, over the past few years, I watched that boundary gradually disappear.

AI-generated images became more convincing. AI coding assistants became more capable. Models began to understand increasingly complex instructions and produce results that previously required human expertise.

At first, I believed the true transformation of AI was still far away. I thought AI would become more powerful, but there would always be a space reserved for human creators.

But seeing this rapid progress changed my perspective.

I have to admit that it is frustrating to watch AI accomplish so much in such a short time. Yet, even in an era where AI can create almost anything, I still want to become a **game designer**, a **game artist**, and continue learning programming and other skills.

So I started asking myself:

**Why?**

I asked DeepSeek this question as well. It suggested that pure visual artists and pure logic programmers may no longer be enough. Instead, humans should focus on defining *game feel*, for example, making movement feel heavier, smoother, or more realistic through precise design decisions.

But I do not fully agree.

If AI can eventually understand concepts like "heaviness," "fluidity," and "danger," and translate them into physics, timing, animation, and state-machine logic, then this boundary may disappear too.

So the deeper question is not whether AI can replace certain skills.

It is:

> **When machines become capable of creating almost everything, why do humans still need to learn and create?**

---

## Beyond the Ability Race

Before this reflection, my answer was simpler.

As a design student, I believed learning programming was practical. It allows me to prototype ideas, communicate with programmers, understand technical limitations, and evaluate AI-generated code.

These reasons are still true.

However, after witnessing how quickly AI improved, I realised that practical usefulness alone is not enough. If programming is only a production skill, then it will eventually become another competition humans may lose.

This made me reconsider what learning programming actually means.

We often frame the problem as:

> **Human abilities vs. AI abilities**

But perhaps this framework itself is the problem.

Boden (1998) showed that AI can generate novel combinations, explore conceptual spaces, and transform existing ideas. The boundary between human and machine creativity has never been fixed.

Therefore, instead of asking:

> What can humans do that AI cannot?

we should ask:

> What does creating mean when creation itself becomes cheap?

---

## Why the Ability Race Is the Problem

Heidegger (1954/1977) argued that modern technology is not merely a neutral tool. Through **Enframing** (*Ge-stell*), technology encourages us to see the world through calculation, optimisation, and efficiency.

When we ask "What can AI do, and what can humans still do?", we may already accept this logic. We measure human value using the same standards as machine performance.

If every ability can eventually be analysed and optimised, then searching for a final uniquely human ability will never succeed.

AI did not only change what I could produce.

It changed the questions I asked.

At first:

> How can AI help me create faster?

Later:

> If AI can create faster than me, why should I create?

Perhaps technology does not only change our tools. It changes how we understand ourselves.

---

## Creation Is More Than Production

In fact, the challenge created by AI is not completely new. Many years before, Benjamin have argued that mechanical reproduction challenged the uniqueness and "aura" of artworks.

Even if AI could create something more "Van Gogh" than Van Gogh himself, we would still care that a certain artwork was created by Van Gogh, not simply because it is better, but because it is connected to a particular human life.

This does not mean AI can never have consciousness or experience. Such claims are difficult to prove. However, simulation and lived experience are not necessarily the same.

Human beings have limited time, memories, and experiences. We have only one childhood, one history, and one sequence of choices.

This limitation gives our creations meaning.

---


## Choose, Not Produce 
If AI can produce things better than we can, perhaps the artist's role shifts:
> **The one who, among infinite possibilities, chooses one and takes responsibility for it.** 

This is more than aesthetic judgment. Even if AI can tell us which option is "better," we can still say:
> **"I choose this because I love it."** 

AI can write code. But when *I* learn to code, I am not merely learning to produce code. I am learning how systems are constructed.

Papert (1980) treated programming as a way of thinking〞something through which we can construct and explore ideas. For a game designer, this matters because games are systems of rules, interaction, and emergence (Salen & Zimmerman, 2003). Their rules can even become a form of expression: Bogost (2007) calls this **procedural rhetoric**. So programming is not only implementation.
It is a language for constructing worlds. The value of learning it is not that I can code faster than AI. It is that I can understand what a system *is*, what it *could become*, and why I might want it to exist.

---

## Conclusion: Why Are We Here?

Kazutaka Kodaka once joked that even if AI could do everything, humans would still fail, become jealous, complain, and make mistakes.

These sound like flaws.

But perhaps these imperfections are precisely what make us human.

The value of creation is not only the final result. It is also the fact that a finite person chose, experienced, struggled, and left a trace in the world.

So *why are **we** here?* 

Not to do what AI cannot do. Not to win a race against machines. But to **choose to become ourselves in a world where almost anything can be created**. 
To fail, to love, to become jealous, to lose our temper〞and still choose to create. To know that AI may do it "better," and still put our finite lives into the work. **To become oneself.**
